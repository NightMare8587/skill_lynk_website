What's Your Interview Readiness Score? Why We Don't Show You Just One

Search "interview readiness score" and you'll find a dozen apps happy to hand you a number — 72/100, a letter grade, a progress ring. SkillLynk deliberately doesn't do that on your own Skill Passport. Here's the actual reasoning, not a marketing dodge.

A single score optimizes for the wrong thing

The moment an app shows you one composite readiness number, you stop practicing to get better and start practicing to move the number. That's not hypothetical — it's the same failure mode game-style matchmaking ratings have had for decades: once a player can see their MMR, some fraction of them start playing to protect the number instead of playing well. A "readiness score" built from practice volume, streaks, and a handful of scored sessions is easy to nudge upward without the underlying skill actually improving — do ten easy drills in a row, watch the number climb, feel ready, walk into a real interview, and get exactly the surprise the score was supposed to prevent.

The one place a raw score does exist — and why you never see it

If you've enrolled in a company-run Hiring Drive, that specific company does see a peak and average score for your rounds, plus a panel recommendation. That number exists for one reason: it's the input to a real hiring decision that company is making right now, about that specific role. It is never shown back to you — not because it's hidden for effect, but because a number computed for someone else's decision has no honest meaning as a verdict on you. A `do_not_advance` recommendation for a Series B backend role tells you nothing about whether you're ready for a different company's frontend role next month, and showing it to you would just be discouraging noise dressed up as feedback.

What your own Skill Passport shows instead

Your public, shareable Skill Passport — the one you control whether to turn on, and the one a company can discover you through — reports real platform activity instead of a score:

  • Topics demonstrated — how many distinct skill areas you've built real mastery in, not just attempted once
  • Interviews completed — peer sessions and AI voice interviews you've actually finished, across every format, not one you happened to do well on
  • Coding problems solved — real, fully-passed submissions, not attempts
  • Hiring Drives completed — as a plain fact, with no score or verdict attached

Nobody can inflate this by gaming a formula. Every number on it is a count of something that either happened or didn't. A company evaluating you through Skill Passport sees the same honest breadth signal you'd get from a real reference check — "this person has actually done the work across these areas" — instead of a single number that invites the question "compared to what, exactly?"

So where do you actually see your own detail?

Your private growth dashboard — visible only to you, never shared or discoverable — is where the granular picture lives: per-topic mastery scores, your recent Drive round results if you've taken any, and a week-by-week view of your own practice activity. This is deliberately the opposite audience from your public passport: it's for your own motivation and planning, not for anyone evaluating you, so it can afford to show the messier, more specific detail a single public score would flatten away.

The honest answer to "am I ready?"

There isn't a number that answers that question responsibly, for the same reason no single number on a resume ever has. What actually answers it: whether you've been tested across the topics the role actually needs, in a format close to the real thing (a live, spoken, back-and-forth conversation — not a multiple-choice quiz), recently enough that the skill is current. That's a breadth-and-recency question, not a leaderboard question, and it's exactly what topics-demonstrated and interviews-completed are built to answer instead of papering over with a fake precision score.
