Sales Interview Questions: 10 You'll Be Asked, and How to Answer Them

A sales interview is the only interview where the interview itself is the job sample. While you answer, the interviewer is watching how you sell, listen and handle being pushed back on. The good news: the questions barely change between companies, whether it's SaaS, edtech, banking, insurance or FMCG.

What a sales interviewer is checking

  • Do you listen before you talk? The best salespeople ask more than they pitch.
  • Can you handle "no"? Most calls and meetings in sales end without a sale. They want to see you stay steady.
  • Are you driven by targets? And do you talk about your results in numbers?
  • Do you have a process? Selling is a repeatable system, not just charm.
  • Are you coachable? Every sales team trains its own way of selling.

Here are the questions you're most likely to get, with what works and what doesn't.

1. "Tell me about yourself."

In a sales interview, this is your first pitch, and the product is you. Keep it to about a minute, and put at least one number in it. Numbers are the language of sales.

If you're a fresher, use anything where you persuaded people or chased a goal:

"I'm a final-year BBA student. The experience I'm proudest of is running sponsorships for our college fest: I made about 40 calls to local businesses, got meetings with 12, and closed 8 sponsors worth ₹1.2 lakh in total, our highest ever. That's when I realised I enjoy the whole process, from the first call to the yes, and it's why I'm applying for this inside sales role."

If you're already in sales, lead with your results: what you sold, to whom, your target and how you did against it over the last year or two.

2. "Why sales?"

"I'm a people person" is what everyone says, and "for the incentives" alone worries interviewers about how long you'll stay when a quarter goes badly. Better answers combine two things: you like the challenge of a clear target, and you've already enjoyed some version of the work (convincing people, solving a customer's problem, competing). Give an example of one.

3. "Sell me this pen."

The classic, and it's still asked. The trap is to start listing features ("it's smooth, it's light, it's blue"). The interviewer is checking whether you ask before you pitch. A good structure:

  1. Ask questions. "When did you last use a pen? What for? What annoys you about the pens you use?"
  2. Connect the product to what they said. "You said you sign client documents all day and pens keep running out at the worst moment. This one has a refill that lasts…"
  3. Ask for the close. "Shall I put one in your bag for tomorrow's meetings?"

Asking for the sale at the end matters. Many candidates do a good job of the first two steps and then just stop talking.

4. "Your product is too expensive."

The interviewer may play the customer and throw objections at you. Don't argue and don't jump to a discount. Use a simple sequence:

  • Acknowledge: "That's fair, it's a real investment."
  • Clarify: "Too expensive compared with what: another option you're looking at, or your budget for this?"
  • Reframe on value: tie the price to what they told you earlier: time saved, revenue gained, a problem gone.
  • Check: "Does that change how you see the price?"

The clarifying question is the step most people skip, and it's the one that tells you what the real objection is.

5. "Tell me about a target you missed."

Everyone in sales misses sometimes, so "I've never missed" isn't believable. Use the STAR structure (situation, task, action, result) and spend most of the answer on what you changed:

"In my second quarter I hit 70% of target. When I went through my pipeline, I saw I'd spent too long on large leads that weren't ready to buy. I started qualifying harder in the first call and kept my pipeline at three times my target. The next two quarters I reached 104% and 112%."

Freshers can use an academic or project goal they fell short of. Our guide to behavioral questions and the STAR method shows how to build a small set of stories for questions like this.

6. "How do you handle rejection?"

Show that you don't take a "no" personally, and that you learn from it. Something like: "I remind myself that a no is usually about timing or fit, not about me. I note why they said no, because patterns there tell me what to fix, and I make the next call straight away instead of dwelling on it." A real example of bouncing back is better than any general statement.

7. "Walk me through your sales process." / "How would you prioritise 100 leads?"

Interviewers want to see that you think in stages: prospect, qualify, discover needs, present, handle objections, close, follow up. For prioritising, talk about qualifying: who has the need, the budget and the authority to buy, and how soon. Then go after the most likely and largest opportunities first, without letting the rest go cold.

Freshers aren't expected to know a company's process, but knowing these stages and words like "pipeline", "conversion rate" and "CRM" shows you've done your homework.

8. The numbers question

Many sales interviews include a quick funnel calculation, like this:

Your monthly target is ₹10 lakh. An average deal is ₹50,000. You close 20% of the product demos you give, and 25% of your calls book a demo. How many calls do you need to make in a month?

StepWorkingResult
Deals needed₹10,00,000 ÷ ₹50,00020 deals
Demos needed20 ÷ 20%100 demos
Calls needed100 ÷ 25%400 calls

Answer: 400 calls, about 20 a working day. Then say what you'd improve first: lifting the demo-to-close rate from 20% to 25% cuts the calls needed from 400 to 320. Showing you think about which lever to pull is what makes this answer stand out.

9. "What do you know about our product and customers?"

Before the interview, find out what they sell, who buys it, and who they compete with. If you can, use the product or go through its sign-up flow. Then come with one observation: "I noticed your pricing page pushes the annual plan. Do most customers start monthly?" It shows exactly the curiosity they're hiring for.

10. "Do you have any questions for us?"

In sales, this is also part of the test. Good questions:

  • "What percentage of the team hit target last quarter?"
  • "How long is the ramp-up before a new person carries a full target?"
  • "Where do leads come from: marketing, or mostly our own prospecting?"
  • "How is the incentive structured?"

The answers also tell you a lot about whether the job is realistic.

If there's a role-play

Many sales interviews include a cold-call or meeting role-play. Keep a simple structure for a cold call: introduce yourself, ask for a moment of their time, give one reason you're calling that matters to them, ask a question, and agree a next step (a demo, a meeting, a follow-up). It's fine to pause and think. Rushing to fill silence is a common mistake, in role-plays and real calls.

Freshers and experienced candidates

  • Freshers: nobody expects sales results yet. They want evidence of the traits: fest sponsorships, fundraising, a part-time job with customers, a debate or a competition you prepared hard for.
  • Experienced: know your numbers precisely: target and attainment, average deal size, sales cycle length, who you sold to. Expect follow-up questions on them, and never inflate them. Interviewers check, and references do too.

How to practise

Sales interviews are spoken, often with interruptions and pushback, so practising in your head doesn't prepare you. Say your answers out loud, get someone to throw objections at you, and do the funnel maths until it's quick.
