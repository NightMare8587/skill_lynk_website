What an AI Mock Interview Actually Feels Like

"AI interview" gets used for everything from a chatbot quiz to a recorded one-way video prompt. This is about the specific thing SkillLynk does: a live, spoken, back-and-forth conversation with an AI interviewer — and what it's actually like to sit through one.

It's a call, not a form

You don't type answers. You open the app, start a session, and within a few seconds you're talking — out loud, over your microphone — to an interviewer that responds in real time. It asks a question, you answer, it follows up based on what you actually said, not a pre-written script. If you mention you used a queue to handle retries, it might ask why a queue instead of a simple retry loop. That's the part that surprises people the first time: it's genuinely listening to the content of your answer, not just checking whether you said a keyword.

The awkward part, honestly

The first thirty seconds feel strange. Talking to an AI that sounds conversational but isn't a person takes a moment to adjust to — most people pause more than they would with a human interviewer, second-guess whether it "understood" them, or over-explain out of habit. That fades fast. By the second or third question, it starts feeling more like thinking out loud on a call with someone who's actually paying attention, mostly because the follow-ups are specific enough that it's clear something is tracking what you said.

What it's actually evaluating

A live session isn't graded on keyword matching. The conversation is transcribed and scored against a rubric that looks at things like:

  • Whether you actually answered the question that was asked, not a related one
  • How you reasoned through trade-offs when there wasn't one obviously correct answer
  • Whether your explanation held up when the interviewer pushed back or asked "why not X instead"
  • How clearly you communicated under the mild real-time pressure of a live conversation

That's a meaningfully different bar than a multiple-choice quiz, which can only ever check whether you recognize a correct answer among a few options. A live conversation can tell the difference between someone who memorized a definition and someone who understands why the definition is true.

Where it falls short of a human interviewer

Worth being direct about this: it's not a replacement for feedback from someone who's actually hired for the role you want. It won't tell you "this is exactly what threw me off in my last interview loop," because it doesn't have that specific context. What it's good for is volume and availability — practicing a technical conversation at 11pm before an interview, or running the same topic five times in a week to get comfortable with the format, without needing to schedule around another person's calendar. Peer mock interviews and AI voice interviews aren't competing features on SkillLynk; they're suited to different moments — a peer session when you want a human's read on your delivery, an AI session when you want to drill a topic on your own schedule.

How to actually get value out of one

The sessions that go well tend to share a pattern: treat the follow-up questions as real signal, not an obstacle to get past. If the interviewer asks why you'd choose one approach over another, that's usually pointing at the exact thing an interview panel would also probe. Answering "because that's just what I know" is a worse answer than genuinely thinking through the trade-off out loud, even if it takes you a few extra seconds — the scoring reflects that.

It also helps to treat a bad session as data rather than a verdict. A live AI interview scores what happened in that specific conversation; a rough one on a topic you're weak in is exactly the situation practice exists for.
